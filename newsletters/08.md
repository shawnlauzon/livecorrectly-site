Hey {{firstName}},

I will always remember the first day of freshman psychology class. The professor asked half of the class to cover their ears and told them about a study which recently confirmed the phrase "If at first you don't succeed, try, try again." Then she asked the other half to cover their ears and told them that this phrase was confirmed: "Don't throw good money after bad".

Then she asked the entire class about a different scenario and what they think they should do. And as you might expect, the first half thought they should keep trying, and the second half thought they should quit.

## The pattern is everywhere

We see this all over the place. And it's all _common sense_:

- Look before you leap / He who hesitates is lost
- Many hands make light work / Too many cooks spoil the broth
- Absence makes the heart grow fonder / Out of sight, out of mind

So which is it?

Of course, it depends. Most people think it depends only on the situation.

But more importantly, it depends on YOU.

## Your design tells you which

Here's what I think happened. A long time ago, some guy with a design to Wait for Clarity started preaching the gospel of "Look before you leap", and some people who happened to have the same design latched on, it worked for them, and they made millions of dollars. The other people, who didn't have that design, got frustrated and beat themselves up and you never heard from them again.

At the same time a different guy, an Initiator, preached the gospel of "He who hesitates is lost", and same story: some people were successful, and some weren't.

## The one I got wrong

In 2022 I was building my coaching practice, and the advice that I read was _Consistency is king_: every day post something on social media, and that's how you get an audience. And I had lots of things to say: whenever I talked with someone I couldn't shut up. And so I sat down with the phone in front of me, ready to drop some wisdom, and then:

Nothing.

Total mind blank. So I started writing it down first and recorded it: flat. So I got a teleprompter app and recorded content like that. It lasted about 2 weeks before I was completely burnt out.

Because the proverb that really works for me is _Quality over quantity_.

And so I have enough energy to write something really good and send it out to you every week. It actually takes _more_ time than the daily things I was doing!

But I have a week for my gut to tell me when it has energy, and that's the moment that I do the work.

## But how do I know the difference?

Someone wrote back after the last email with the best question I've gotten in a while. I'm paraphrasing:

> Learning to tell the difference between intuition and avoidance is probably one of the hardest parts.

She's right, and here's why it's so hard: when you don't know which sense is common to you, your mind picks whichever one gets you off the hook today.

Tired? _Sleep on it_. Bored of waiting? _He who hesitates is lost._ Scared? _Better safe than sorry._ Restless? _Nothing ventured, nothing gained._

So which of these should you trust?

**Nothing that your mind comes up with can be trusted.**

As I said last week, it's not that it is necessarily incorrectly. You just can't trust it when it comes from your mind.

## Tricks of the mind

Just a few of the ways the mind tries to get us to do (or not to do) something:

Obligation:

- I should ...
- I have to ...

Comparison:

- Everyone else is ...
- Successful people ...

Urgency (marketers love this):

- This is my last chance to ...
- If I don't act now, I'll miss out...

Reasonableness:

- I can just try to ...
- It's easy for AI to just ...

Thinking I am the problem (often tag teams with comparison)

- I'm being lazy.
- It's good for me to ...

Avoidance dressed as wisdom:

- I don't have the energy now.
- I should do more research first.

{% if authority=='emotional' %}

### Neither proverb is yours in the moment, because you don't have a moment.

You are designed to have no truth available right now. Not less truth — none. Whatever you feel about a decision at 2pm is one frame of a movie, and the movie is not over. If you feel certain, that's the wave, not the answer. If you feel hopeless, also the wave.

Your version of the proverb is the boring one: sleep on it. Then sleep on it again.

The hard part isn't waiting. It's that waiting looks like avoidance from the outside, and people will tell you so. It's also that the feeling of clarity at the top of the wave is extremely convincing. That's the one to watch.

Practical version: when someone asks you for an answer, say "I'll get back to you tomorrow." Every time. That sentence is most of your design.

{% elsif authority=='sacral' %}

### "He who hesitates is lost" is closer to yours — but not the way people mean it.

You do have an answer available right now, and it's a response, not a decision. It's a sound and a movement before it's a thought — the uh-huh or the un-un, the lift or the drop. It's already happened by the time you notice it.

What loses it isn't hesitation. It's reasoning. The moment you start weighing pros and cons, you're out of the thing that actually knows.

So the question to ask is never "should I?" It's a yes-or-no question, asked out loud if you can manage it, and then you listen for what your body does before your mind gets involved.

The trap: your mind will construct a very good case for the thing your gut already declined. It will sound responsible. It will mention money.

{% elsif authority=='splenic' %}

### "He who hesitates is lost" was written by someone built like you.

Yours is instant, quiet, and it says it once. It doesn't repeat, it doesn't argue, and it doesn't come back later with a better version. Miss it and it's gone.

Which means your risk is the opposite of most people's. You're not in danger of acting too fast — you're in danger of talking yourself out of something you already knew, because it came so quietly that it didn't seem like enough to go on.

The tell: yours has no volume. If it's loud, insistent, or repeating, that's fear wearing your intuition's coat.

{% elsif authority=='ego' %}

### Your question isn't "is this right?" It's "do I want it?"

You're built to decide from will and desire, which our culture has trained you to be embarrassed about. Wanting something is treated as a weak reason. For you it's the only reason that holds up.

The proverb you need isn't in the list. It's closer to: don't promise what you don't want to do. Your commitments hold when the wanting is real and collapse when they were made to be agreeable.

{% elsif authority=='self-projected' %}

### You can't find this one by thinking. You find it by talking.

Your truth comes out in your own voice — which means you need another person in the room, not for their advice but for their ears. You'll hear which way you're going as you say it out loud.

Pick your sounding boards carefully. You need people who will let you ramble without steering.

{% elsif authority=='mental' %}

### You don't have an authority in your body, and that's not a deficiency.

You're designed to talk it through with trusted people and to pay close attention to where you are when you do. Environment does more for your clarity than any amount of analysis.

The proverbs are both wrong for you. Yours is: get out of the room and go somewhere that feels right, then talk.

{% elsif authority=='lunar' %}

### Neither, and it's not close.

Yours runs on about 28 days, which sounds absurd until you notice you've already been doing it — the decisions you rushed are the ones you regret, and the ones you let sit came out fine.

This isn't hesitation. It's the correct length of your process, and nearly everyone around you will misread it.

{% endif %}
